# Logitech Harmony Alternatives: Best Universal Remotes for HTPC in 2026

For over a decade, the Logitech Harmony line was the default answer to "how do I control my whole home theater with one remote?" Then, in April 2021, Logitech announced it would stop manufacturing Harmony remotes. The company promised to keep the cloud service and app running "as long as customers are using it," but with no new hardware, dwindling stock, and an aging device database, Harmony is now a slowly sinking ship.

If your trusty Harmony 650, Elite, or Companion finally dies — or you're building a new HTPC setup and want a single remote to rule your TV, receiver, streaming box, and media PC — you need a plan that doesn't depend on discontinued hardware. The good news: several strong alternatives have matured, and some of them are genuinely better than Harmony ever was for specific use cases.

This guide breaks down the real options in 2026, what each one does well, and which one fits your setup.

## Why Harmony Is Hard to Replace

Before jumping to alternatives, it helps to understand what made Harmony special. It wasn't just an IR blaster. Harmony combined three things that are surprisingly hard to find in one product:

- **A massive device database.** Harmony's cloud held IR codes for hundreds of thousands of devices, so setup was mostly "type your model number."
- **Activity-based control.** Instead of controlling one device at a time, you pressed "Watch Plex" and the remote powered on the TV, switched the receiver to the right input, turned on the HTPC, and mapped every button to the right target.
- **Hybrid IR + Bluetooth + IP control.** Harmony Hub could control legacy IR gear and modern network devices, including some HTPC software over the network.

Any replacement has to cover most of those bases. Here's how the field stacks up.

## SofaBaton X1S: The Closest Thing to a Modern Harmony

If you want the Harmony experience — a real remote, activity buttons, and a hub that speaks IR, Bluetooth, and Wi-Fi — the SofaBaton X1S is the obvious heir. It ships with a hub that blasts IR around your cabinet (so devices don't need line-of-sight), pairs over Bluetooth to game consoles and some streamers, and configures through a phone app with a large device database.

Its killer feature is activity control done properly. You define a "Watch Movie" activity, and one press sequences power, inputs, and button mapping across every device. There's even a small screen on the remote for switching activities. For a living-room HTPC that lives alongside a TV, AV receiver, and a streaming stick, this is the least compromised option.

The trade-offs: it's the most expensive choice, the app has a learning curve, and like all cloud-assisted remotes, you're once again trusting a company to keep its database alive. SofaBaton has been actively updating the platform, which is more than can be said for Harmony.

## SofaBaton U2: The Budget One-Remote Fix

Not everyone needs a hub. If your gear is stacked in an open cabinet with clear line-of-sight, the SofaBaton U2 is a pure IR universal remote that controls up to five devices, supports basic macros, and costs a fraction of the X1S. It won't do Bluetooth or network control, so it's a poor fit if your HTPC relies on a wireless keyboard-style input — but paired with an IR receiver like a [Flirc USB dongle](/remotes/flirc-usb-setup-guide/), it can drive a media PC surprisingly well.

Think of the U2 as the spiritual successor to the Harmony 665: simple, affordable, no cloud lock-in beyond initial setup.

## Broadlink RM4 Pro: For Tinkerers and Smart-Home Fans

The Broadlink RM4 Pro takes a completely different approach. It's a small Wi-Fi hub that learns IR (and RF) codes, then exposes them to apps, voice assistants, and — crucially — home automation platforms like Home Assistant. There's no physical remote in the box; your "remote" becomes your phone, a dashboard, or a voice command.

For HTPC owners already running Home Assistant or Node-RED, this is powerful. You can build an automation where "movie night" dims the lights, closes the blinds, powers the TV and receiver, and launches your media server's app — all triggered by one tap or phrase. It's the most flexible option here and the cheapest hub, but it demands the most setup effort and offers no dedicated handheld remote out of the box.

## SwitchBot Universal Remote: The Matter-Era Option

SwitchBot's universal remote is a newer entrant built for households that have gone all-in on smart home. It combines a physical remote with a hub that handles IR and Bluetooth, and it plays nicely with Alexa, Google Home, and Siri via Matter. If you already own SwitchBot blinds, plugs, or lights, consolidating their control alongside your home theater into one remote is genuinely convenient.

For a pure HTPC-only setup it's overkill, but as the center of a connected living room it's a smart pick — and it sidesteps the "one company controls the database" risk somewhat by leaning on open standards like Matter.

## Don't Overlook HDMI-CEC and the HTPC Itself

Here's the option most people forget: you may not need a universal remote at all. Modern TVs support **HDMI-CEC**, which lets a single remote pass commands to connected devices over the HDMI cable. If your HTPC, streaming box, and TV all support CEC, your existing TV remote can often navigate the media PC directly. It's free, requires no extra hardware, and eliminates a device from your setup. We cover the details in our [HDMI-CEC explainer](/remotes/hdmi-cec-explained-one-remote/).

Similarly, if your goal is purely to drive the HTPC — not the TV and receiver — a dedicated input device is simpler and cheaper than any universal remote. An [air mouse remote](/remotes/air-mouse-remotes-mini-pc/) handles pointer-based interfaces, and there's a strong case for [using your smartphone as the remote](/remotes/smartphone-remote-apps-htpc/) with the official Plex, Jellyfin, or Kodi apps.

### When a Universal Remote Still Wins

Universal remotes earn their keep when you're controlling **three or more devices with different inputs and power states** — for example a TV, an AV receiver, a game console, and an HTPC. That's the scenario where activity macros save real friction and where CEC alone falls short. If you're only touching one or two devices, spend the money elsewhere in your build.

## How to Migrate Off a Dying Harmony

If your Harmony still works but you're planning ahead, a few practical steps make the transition smoother:

1. **Document your activities.** Screenshot every activity's device sequence and button mapping from the Harmony app while it still works. This is your blueprint for reconfiguring a replacement.
2. **Note your device model numbers.** Any new remote will ask for them. Having the exact model of each TV, receiver, and box speeds setup dramatically.
3. **Decide hub vs. line-of-sight.** If your gear is hidden in a closed cabinet, prioritize a hub-based system (SofaBaton X1S, SwitchBot, or Broadlink). If everything is visible, an IR-only remote is cheaper and simpler.
4. **Test CEC first.** Before buying anything, enable HDMI-CEC on your TV and see how much your setup can already do with the remote you own.

## Bottom Line: Which Should You Buy?

- **Want the closest Harmony replacement with a real remote and activities?** Get the **SofaBaton X1S**. It's the most complete package and the safest recommendation for most HTPC owners.
- **On a budget with line-of-sight gear?** The **SofaBaton U2** does the job for a fraction of the price.
- **Already running Home Assistant or love tinkering?** The **Broadlink RM4 Pro** gives you unmatched flexibility for the lowest cost — as long as you're comfortable building the automations.
- **Deep into smart home and Matter?** The **SwitchBot Universal Remote** consolidates everything into one ecosystem.
- **Only controlling the HTPC?** Skip universal remotes entirely — try HDMI-CEC, an air mouse, or your phone.

Harmony's death felt like the end of an era, but the universal remote isn't dead. In some ways, the alternatives are more capable and less locked-in than what Logitech left behind. Pick based on how many devices you're wrangling, and you'll end up with a setup that outlasts anything Harmony could have offered.
